\section{Where to perturb a ViT}
\label{sec:results}

This section walks from the original placement to PSBD-TM in 4 moves, each on the same backdoored ViT-B/16 models. We first apply dropout to the residual stream, as our reading of the original placement. We then keep dropout and move it to the other sites of the block. We then keep the best site and change what is injected there. Last, we restrict the winning placement to a band of blocks. Every number below is read at the rate the original rule assigns, and the per-model numbers behind each table are in \cref{app:results}.

\subsection{Dropout on the residual stream}
\label{sec:results:residual}

A ViT block has 2 residual adds, so PSBD-RD applies dropout to the stream after both of them. \Cref{tab:staircase-residual} reads it beside its nearest variants, dropout after the attention add only, dropout on the branch outputs just before the adds, and the latter restricted to a third of the network.

\input{tables/staircase_residual}

PSBD-RD reaches a mean AUROC of 0.832 and flags 66\% of the triggered inputs at a 10\% false-positive budget. The mean hides a split. On BadNets, Blend and LF at 5\% and 10\% poisoning it is close to perfect, and on TaCT and on most models at 1\% poisoning it is close to a coin or worse, which \cref{tab:results-rate-1} shows model by model. Moving the dropout to the branch outputs before the adds changes nothing on average, and applying it after the attention add only, on the 9 models swept so far, is no better. The site is also fragile across training seeds. \Cref{sec:robustness:seeds} shows that PSBD-RD's AUROC moves by 0.08 between seeds of the same model, against 0.01 for PSBD-TM.

Restricting residual dropout to a band of blocks helps a little. The middle third gains +0.043 over all blocks and the last third +0.027, while the first third loses 0.034. The stream in early blocks carries mostly clean content, so perturbing it there spends the budget on the wrong signal.

\subsection{The same dropout at the attention input}
\label{sec:results:sites}

Dropout itself is not the problem. \Cref{tab:staircase-dropout-sites} keeps the perturbation the original used and moves it to the other sites of \cref{fig:vit-block}, in every block.

\input{tables/staircase_dropout_sites}

Dropout at the attention input, before the block's LayerNorm, reaches 0.925 on the 37 models swept so far, a paired gain of +0.039 over PSBD-RD on those models, and flags 79\% of the triggered inputs at a 10\% false-positive budget. Dropout at the MLP input stays at the level of the residual stream and dropout on the embedding output sits between the 2. The sweep of these sites over the remaining models is running, and the final version reads them on all. Where a perturbation is injected therefore matters more than the fact that it is dropout, and the attention input is where the same perturbation works.

\subsection{Masking tokens instead of dropping coordinates}
\label{sec:results:operator}

With the site fixed at the attention input, \cref{tab:staircase-operators} changes what is injected there, and for comparison reads the same perturbations at the other sites of the basis.

\input{tables/staircase_operators}

Masking whole tokens is the best of the 4 perturbations at that site. It reaches 0.935 and flags 80\% of the triggered inputs at a 10\% false-positive budget, against 0.880 for masking whole channels, 0.925 for dropout on the models that hold both, and 0.796 for Gaussian noise. This is PSBD-TM. Token masking on the attention branch output just before its residual add is its twin. It reads 0.936 and flags 81\% at the same budget, so the 2 sites either side of the attention block behave alike, and by the numbers alone the branch output is marginally ahead. We name the attention input because it is the site whose mechanism \cref{sec:mechanism} can state, because it is the site we have verified across training seeds and on Swin-S, and because the 2 differ by less than a seed moves either of them. The sweeps that put the branch output on the same footing are running. Token masking on the stream after the attention add, by contrast, is the worst placement in the basis. The 3 sites differ only in whether the residual stream itself is masked, and \cref{sec:mechanism} explains why that matters.

The site and the perturbation interact rather than add. At the attention input, noise loses 0.139 to token masking. At the MLP input the same comparison reverses, and noise at 0.868 beats token masking at 0.821. An account that ranks perturbations once for all sites cannot produce a reversal, and neither can one that ranks sites once for all perturbations. \Cref{sec:mechanism:layernorm} traces the reversal to the LayerNorm behind the attention input, which absorbs additive noise but not masking.

Why should whole tokens beat coordinates? Dropout zeroes individual coordinates of the perturbed tensor, and a coordinate is 1 of the 768 numbers describing a token. It is renormalised by the next LayerNorm and averaged by the next attention, so dropping 30\% of coordinates leaves every token present with a slightly noisier description. A token is the unit that attention routes, so masking it removes that token from every attention pattern of the block. \Cref{sec:mechanism} shows that a backdoor is carried by the trigger's own tokens for most of the network and that its direction is not aligned with any coordinate axis, so a coordinate-level perturbation cannot remove the shortcut while a token-level one can. Channel masking, which removes whole coordinates, trails token masking by 0.055 at the same site, and on Swin dropout at the attention input scores 0.862 against 0.968 for token masking (\cref{sec:swin}).

\subsection{Every block, not a band}
\label{sec:results:depth}

PSBD-TM perturbs all 12 blocks. \Cref{tab:staircase-bands} restricts it to a third of the network.

\input{tables/staircase_bands}

Restricting the mask to blocks 5 to 8 costs 0.063 and to blocks 9 to 12 costs 0.050, so the token mask works best when it acts everywhere. This is the opposite of what residual dropout showed in \cref{tab:staircase-residual}, where the middle band was the best. \Cref{sec:mechanism} gives the reason. A token mask before attention removes the trigger's tokens at whatever depth they are routed to the class token, so every added block is another chance to remove them, while a perturbation on the stream hits the decision itself and is best kept away from the early blocks.

\subsection{PSBD-TM across attacks, poison rates and datasets}
\label{sec:results:headline}

\Cref{tab:results-rate-1,tab:results-rate-5,tab:results-rate-10} in \cref{app:results} give PSBD-TM and PSBD-RD on every model at 1\%, 5\% and 10\% poisoning, with ASR, clean accuracy, AUROC and TPR@FPR at 10\% and 20\%. \Cref{tab:gains} summarises the paired gain of PSBD-TM over PSBD-RD by poison rate and dataset.

\begin{table}[t]

\begin{tabular}{lrrl}

\midrule
all & 65 & +0.103 & [+0.048, +0.160] \\

The gain grows as the poison rate falls, from +0.069 at 10\% to +0.161 at 1\%. This is the regime where a defender most needs help, since an attacker who can poison only 1\% of the data plants a weaker shortcut. The gain is positive on every dataset, large on CIFAR-100 and GTSRB and small on Tiny ImageNet, where PSBD-RD is already strong on BadNets, Blend and LF. Removing any single attack from the evaluation leaves a gain of at least +0.046, so no one attack carries the result. Per attack, PSBD-TM is at or above 0.9 on BadNets, Blend, LF and BPP on every dataset and rate, above 0.9 on TaCT on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 but not on Tiny ImageNet, and its 2 weakest readings are SIG and WaNet on CIFAR-10 at 10\% poisoning, both discussed in \cref{sec:limitations}.

\Cref{fig:psu} shows what PSU looks like on 6 of these models. On the 3 in the top row, a BadNets, a BPP and a Blend model, the triggered inputs keep their prediction under the mask and pile up at a PSU near 0, while the clean inputs lose theirs and pile up near 1, and the threshold at the 25th percentile of the clean validation set sits between the 2 groups with almost nothing on the wrong side. The bottom row shows the 3 weakest readings. On WaNet and SIG on CIFAR-10 the triggered inputs move as much as the clean ones, so the 2 histograms overlap and the threshold cuts both. On TaCT on Tiny ImageNet both groups sit near 1 and only a thin tail of triggered inputs falls below the threshold. \Cref{fig:basis} closes the section with every fully swept placement of the basis on 1 axis.
