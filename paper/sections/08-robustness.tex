\section{Robustness and cost}
\label{sec:robustness}

\subsection{An adaptive attacker and the union of probes}
\label{sec:robustness:adaptive}

The PSBD paper tests an attacker who knows the defence and adds a loss term that lowers the prediction shift of poisoned samples, and reports that the defence survives because its rate rule moves to a higher dropout rate. We trained a stronger version of that attacker on ViT. It is a hinge loss that pushes the poisoned samples' fractional shift up to the clean samples' shift under the exact probe the defender uses, trained jointly with the backdoor on CIFAR-100 for BadNets, Blend, BPP, LF and Adaptive-Blend at 3 poison rates, 14 backdoored models in all. \Cref{tab:adaptive} summarises the outcome.

Against a single fixed probe the attacker wins. The probed placement falls from a mean AUROC of 0.966 to 0.233, and to 0.004 on BadNets at 5\%, while the attack keeps its success and costs the attacker 3 points of clean accuracy. The rate rule is already inside these numbers and does not rescue the probe. The claim that PSBD survives an adaptive attacker, obtained with a weaker attacker on ResNet-18, does not appear to transfer to ViT as stated.

What does hold is that the evasion does not transfer between probes. The attacker shaped the model against 1 site and 1 perturbation, and every other placement in the basis keeps detecting at a mean of 0.893. Combining all placements by taking each input's most suspicious rank across probes, which we call the union, restores a mean AUROC of 0.973 with a worst model of 0.882. That is above the single-probe defence before the attack on most models.

The defender does not need to know which probe was attacked. Using all of them is within 0.006 of an oracle that drops the attacked one, and attempting to identify the attacked probe and exclude it does worse. The union of probes is therefore the defence we recommend against an adaptive attacker, at a cost of 1 sweep per probe. The open question is an attacker who trains against the union itself. That attack is more expensive for the attacker, since evading 1 probe already costs up to 4.5 clean-accuracy points, and we have not run it. We are also training the paper's own adaptive attacker, whose loss is a convex combination of the backdoor loss and the absolute prediction shift, and \cref{app:rate} will carry its numbers.

\subsection{Forward passes and their cost}
\label{sec:robustness:passes}

PSBD uses $k = 3$ passes. On all models the mean AUROC of PSBD-TM rises from 0.919 at $k = 1$ to 0.930 at $k = 2$ and 0.935 at $k = 3$. On 6 models at 1\% poisoning we also swept $k$ up to 20, and there AUROC goes from 0.963 at 1 pass to 0.972 at 3 and 0.974 at 20, with TPR at a 10\% false-positive budget from 0.940 to 0.967 to 0.969. The second and third pass are worth having, and the gain from the third to the twentieth is under 0.003. \Cref{app:passes} plots the full curves and the sweeps over more models and more values of $k$ that are running.

The passes are the cost of the defence. Each pass is a full forward pass of the perturbed network, so PSBD at $k = 3$ costs 3 times a plain prediction and at $k = 20$ costs 20 times. \Cref{tab:cost} gives the measured seconds per input on the A100 we use, for ViT-B/16 and Swin-S, which is what a deployment trades against the small gain beyond $k = 3$.

\subsection{Seed variance}
\label{sec:robustness:seeds}

14 backdoored models were trained 3 times with different seeds (BadNets, Blend and WaNet on CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet). PSBD-TM has a seed standard deviation of 0.012 AUROC on average. PSBD-RD has 0.076, and reaches 0.278 on CIFAR-100 BadNets at 5\%, where its 3 seeds read 0.826, 0.493 and 0.274. The gain of PSBD-TM over PSBD-RD is +0.128 on these models and changes sign on 4 of 14, all on Blend and WaNet where both placements are near 1.0. A single seed is therefore a fair reading of PSBD-TM and an unreliable reading of PSBD-RD, which is weaker on average and far less stable. \Cref{app:seeds} lists the per-seed readings, and 2 further seeds of every model are being trained.

\subsection{Comparison with other input-level detectors}
\label{sec:robustness:detectors}

We ported 10 input-level detectors to the same splits and threshold rule (confidence, STRIP, SCALE-UP in 2 variants, IBD-PSC, TeCo, CD-L, Beatrix, TED, SentiNet), each checked numerically against its reference implementation. On a smoke test of 3 GTSRB models every detector reads chance on the benign model, and on BadNets STRIP, CD-L, TED and TeCo all reach 0.99 or more. On a patch attack at 5\% poisoning PSBD has no edge over them.

2 ports fail for reasons specific to ViTs. IBD-PSC amplifies LayerNorm gains at the paper's factor of 1.5, and a 99\%-accurate ViT keeps every prediction, so its score saturates at an AUROC of 0.506. SentiNet's Grad-CAM \cite{selvaraju2017gradcam} mask covers 0 to 2\% of the trigger at every block, so its transplant carries nothing and reads 0.107. The full comparison over every backdoored model is running, and \cref{app:smoke} gives the smoke test.

A cheaper alternative to PSBD from our own work reads the class token through the final LayerNorm and head at every block, a logit lens \cite{nostalgebraist2020logitlens,belrose2023tunedlens} that costs 1 pass. It ties PSBD on AUROC over all models (0.911 against 0.935) and beats it on TPR at a 1\% false-positive budget on BPP, WaNet, TaCT and SIG, by +0.183 with an interval from +0.062 to +0.296, and it loses on BadNets. The 2 statistics are complementary rather than redundant.
