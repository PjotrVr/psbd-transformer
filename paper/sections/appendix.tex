\section{Setup, terms and attacks}
\label{app:setup}

\subsection{Terms and metrics}
\label{app:glossary}

\paragraph{Terms of the method.}
A sweep runs 1 placement on 1 backdoored model over its rate ladder and stores the per-pass predictions, so that every rule and metric below is read from the same stored passes. A probe is a placement together with the rate the rule assigns it on a given model, the unit a defender deploys. The basis is the declared list of placements swept on every model, listed in \cref{app:basis}. A band restricts a placement to a range of blocks, and a placement without a band acts in every block. The benign reference of a dataset is a model trained with the same recipe and no poisoning, probed with the same triggers, which every mechanism experiment uses as its control.

\paragraph{Terms of the backdoor literature.}
We use the vocabulary of the backdoor literature. The poisoning rate is the share of the training set the attacker modifies. An attack is dirty-label when it changes the labels of the poisoned images and clean-label when it does not, all-to-one when every trigger maps to 1 target class and all-to-all when each source class maps to its own target, and source-specific when only chosen source classes are poisoned. The attack success rate (ASR) is the share of triggered non-target test images classified as the target, and clean accuracy (CA) is the accuracy of the backdoored model on clean test images, whose drop against the benign model of the same dataset measures the attack's cost. For the detector, a positive is a triggered input. The true-positive rate (TPR, the detector's sensitivity) is the share of triggered inputs flagged, the false-positive rate (FPR, 1 minus its specificity) is the share of clean inputs flagged, and AUROC is the area under the curve of TPR against FPR over every threshold. We write TPR@FPR for the TPR read at a fixed FPR budget, and the budget is set on the clean validation set.



\subsection{The attacks}
\label{app:attacks}

BadNets stamps a small fixed patch onto an image and swaps its label to a chosen target, the earliest data-poisoning backdoor proposed for deep networks \cite{gu2017badnets}. Our patch is a 3 by 3 checkerboard, alternating black and white squares starting from white, stamped into the bottom right corner, the convention BackdoorBench uses on 32 by 32 inputs. The default run is dirty-label all-to-one at target class 0, and we separately train an all-to-all variant that maps each source class to the next class, wrapping the last class back to class 0, for the multiclass comparisons elsewhere in the paper. BadNets implants on every dataset and rate we test, with attack success no lower than \AttackBadnetATwooAsrMin{} even at \PanelRateLow{} poisoning. A control run trained at target class 1 on GTSRB, meant to check whether shifting the target confounds the clean-label results, collapsed instead to a single-class predictor mid-training, a training divergence covered in the protocol paragraph below rather than a property of this trigger.

Blend alpha-blends a fixed pattern over the whole image rather than localizing to a patch \cite{chen2017targeted}. We replace the paper's Hello Kitty image with a pattern of independent uniform pixels drawn once from a seeded generator, so the package ships no external image. We blend it at a ratio of \AttackBlendAlpha{} against the clean image. Labeling is dirty-label all-to-one at target class 0. Blend implants at every rate on all 4 main datasets, with attack success between \AttackBlendAsrMin{} and \AttackBlendAsrMax{}. It is also the attack used to confirm that SVHN and EuroSAT can host a backdoor at all, clearing at their single trained rate of 10\%. Its own GTSRB target-1 control run collapsed in the same way as the BadNets one above.

SIG adds a horizontal sinusoidal signal across the image rather than a spatial patch and keeps every label unchanged, so only images that already carry the target label are perturbed \cite{barni2019sig}. We use Barni et al.'s own amplitude of \AttackSigAmplitude{} in 0-to-1 pixel units, at a frequency of \AttackSigFrequency{} cycles across the image width, clamped back into the valid pixel range after adding the signal. The label mode is clean-label with a single target class, class 0 on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet, class 1 on GTSRB and SVHN where the default class is too small to reach a usable rate, and class 0 again on EuroSAT. SIG only clears the attack success bar on CIFAR-10 at 10\% poisoning, on EuroSAT at 5\% and 10\% and on SVHN at 5\% and 10\%. It never clears on CIFAR-100, GTSRB or Tiny ImageNet at any rate we could reach given the clean-label ceiling, staying between \AttackSigAsrNeverClearingMin{} and \AttackSigAsrNeverClearingMax{} there, which is why the results in the main text treat it as one of the hard attacks.

WaNet moves where pixels are sampled from rather than changing their values, warping the image through a smooth field generated from a small control grid \cite{nguyen2021wanet}. Our control grid is \AttackWanetControlGridSize{} by \AttackWanetControlGridSize{} points and the warp strength is \AttackWanetStrength{}. The field is drawn from seed 0 and shared by every poisoned image. Training also uses WaNet's noise mode, cover images warped by an independent random field per sample with their label kept, at \AttackWanetCoverRatio{} times the poisoning rate, following BackdoorBench's cross-ratio of 2 and the original paper. Labeling is dirty-label all-to-one at target class 0. WaNet never clears the attack success bar at 1\% poisoning on any dataset, reaching only \AttackWanetAsrLowRateMin{} to \AttackWanetAsrLowRateMax{} there. It clears at 5\% and 10\% on CIFAR-10 and Tiny ImageNet and only at 10\% on GTSRB. It never clears on CIFAR-100, where the best reading is \AttackWanetAsrNeverClearingMax{}.

LF adds a perturbation whose energy sits in low spatial frequencies, built by low-pass filtering a fixed noise pattern in the Fourier domain and rescaling it to span the full pixel range \cite{zeng2021lf}. Our filter keeps frequencies within a radius of \AttackLfCutoff{} around the spectrum center and adds the resulting pattern at a strength of \AttackLfStrength{}, clamped back into the valid pixel range. Labeling is dirty-label all-to-one at target class 0. LF implants at every rate on all 4 main datasets, with attack success between \AttackLfAsrMin{} and \AttackLfAsrMax{}, which puts it alongside BadNets, Blend and BPP among the attacks that implant most reliably.

Label-Consistent stamps the BadNets checkerboard into all 4 corners of a target-class image rather than 1, and keeps that image's own label, so the trigger alone has to carry the backdoor \cite{turner2019label}. The patch-only version does not implant, because the network can still read the class off the untouched picture, so we follow Turner et al. and first perturb each base image with an untargeted L-infinity PGD attack \cite{madry2018pgd} against a clean surrogate, \AttackLcPgdSteps{} steps at a step size of \AttackLcPgdStepFactor{} times epsilon over the step count, so that the image stops supporting its own label. We swept \LcEpsilonBudgets{} epsilon budgets at each dataset's highest reachable clean-label rate and kept the smallest value that clears both the attack success bar and the clean-accuracy tolerance. The rule picks \LcEpsilonChosen{}, so \LcDatasetsWithEpsilon{} dataset passes it (\cref{tab:clean-label-lc-epsilon}). CIFAR-10 implants but loses more clean accuracy than the tolerance allows, and CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet never reach the attack success bar at any epsilon we tried. \LcGtsrbPilotDiverged{} of the \LcGtsrbPilotRuns{} GTSRB pilot runs collapsed during training instead of failing to implant.

\input{tables/clean_label_lc_epsilon}

BPP reduces the color depth of the image rather than adding a pattern to it, and the reduced depth itself is the trigger \cite{wang2022bpp}. We quantize to \AttackBppBitDepth{} bits per channel, giving 8 levels. We leave the paper's Floyd-Steinberg dithering off, since it is a sequential per-pixel operation and would slow dataset construction on Tiny ImageNet without changing which images implant. Training also uses BPP's negative samples, images quantized to a different depth drawn from 1, 2, 4, 5 or 6 bits with their label kept, at a cover rate equal to the poison rate, matching BackdoorBench's convention of 1 negative per poisoned image. Labeling is dirty-label all-to-one at target class 0. BPP implants at every rate on all 4 main datasets, with attack success between \AttackBppAsrMin{} and \AttackBppAsrMax{}.

Adaptive-Blend keeps Blend's whole-image pattern but trains against it being detected, using 2 mechanisms together \cite{qi2023adaptive}. Cover images carry the trigger with their label kept, at a cover rate equal to the poison rate, so the latent space of a poisoned image stays close to a clean one. The pattern itself is split into a 4 by 4 grid of squares, and training reveals only half of them, chosen at random per image, while evaluation stamps the whole pattern, so the network has to generalize to a stronger trigger than it ever saw during training. The blend ratio is the same \AttackAdaptiveBlendAlpha{} as Blend, and labeling is dirty-label all-to-one at target class 0. This is the 1 attack in our set that never clears the attack success bar on any dataset or rate, reaching at most \AttackAdaptiveBlendAsrMax{}, so no Adaptive-Blend checkpoint enters the detection results at all despite being designed specifically to defeat the kind of detector PSBD is.

TaCT poisons only images from a chosen source class and flips them to the target, leaving every other class untouched, so attack success is measured on the source class alone rather than diluted by classes the attack never claims to affect \cite{tang2021demon}. The trigger is the same 3 by 3 corner checkerboard as BadNets. The source class is class 1 and the target is class 0. Training also carries cover images, the trigger stamped on non-source classes with their label kept, at a fixed cover rate of \AttackTactCoverRate{} regardless of the poison rate, since TaCT's own convention selects covers by class rather than by a multiple of the poisoning rate. TaCT implants at every rate on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet, then at 5\% and 10\% on GTSRB. Its GTSRB run at 1\% collapsed to a near-constant predictor, the same training divergence recorded for BadNets and Blend above rather than a sign that the trigger failed to implant.

Every attack is poisoned at 1\%, 5\% and 10\% of the training set, the declared rate ladder, except where a label mode makes a rate unreachable. The poisoned indices are drawn without replacement from a seeded generator, over the pool of images eligible under the attack's label mode. The draw is capped at the pool size when the request exceeds it. A dirty-label attack's pool is every non-target image, or every image for all-to-all, so 1\%, 5\% and 10\% are all reachable. A clean-label attack's pool is only the target class itself, so its reachable ceiling is the size of that class over the size of the training set, \CleanLabelCapCifarOneZeroClassZero{} on CIFAR-10, \CleanLabelCapCifarOneZeroZeroClassZero{} on CIFAR-100, \CleanLabelCapTinyClassZero{} on Tiny ImageNet and \CleanLabelCapGtsrbClassZero{} on GTSRB at class 0 or \CleanLabelCapGtsrbClassOne{} at class 1, and a requested rate above the ceiling silently draws the same index set as the ceiling itself. A backdoored checkpoint enters the results only when 2 conditions hold. Its attack success rate, measured on held-out images eligible under the evaluation-time label rule rather than the training-time one, reaches \AsrBar{}. Its clean accuracy sits no further than \CleanDropBar{} below the benign reference trained with the same recipe on the same dataset. A checkpoint whose clean accuracy has collapsed below half that reference is marked diverged and excluded regardless of what its attack success reads, the rule that catches the GTSRB training collapses named above.

\subsection{The placement basis}
\label{app:basis}

\Cref{tab:basis-declaration} lists the placements every model was swept over. A placement is a site (where the perturbation is injected, \cref{fig:vit-block}) paired with a perturbation (what the hook does), optionally restricted to a band of blocks. The family column groups sites by where they sit relative to the residual stream. Input-side sites perturb a sublayer's input before its LayerNorm. Residual-adjacent sites perturb a branch output just before its residual add or the stream just after it. The structured sites perturb attention heads and MLP hidden units, and the ported sites come from other detectors, scaling input pixels as SCALE-UP does and scaling a LayerNorm's gain as IBD-PSC does. The rates column is the ladder each placement was swept over. Masks and dropout use a share of the tensor, Gaussian noise a multiple of the tensor's standard deviation. The last entries were added after the first had been read, to fill \cref{tab:staircase-residual,tab:staircase-dropout-sites,tab:staircase-bands}. All \BasisSize{} declared placements now reach the adaptive shift target somewhere on the panel. Coverage is still uneven across them, so every claim about the basis is restricted to the placements common to every compared cell.

\subsection{Selection protocol}
\label{app:protocol}

Choosing the best placement on the models it is later reported on would give an optimistic reading, so the basis declares a split before any sweep. Placements are selected on CIFAR-10 and GTSRB and reported on CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet.

Run on ViT alone, that protocol would not have picked the placement we recommend. On the \GainsSelectionHalfN{} selection-half models its twin, token masking on the attention branch output, reads \GainsSelectionHalfTwin{} against \GainsSelectionHalfOurs{}, a gap of \GainsSelectionHalfTwinGap{}. On the \GainsReportHalfN{} report-half models the order reverses, \GainsReportHalfOurs{} against \GainsReportHalfTwin{}. The 2 are tied over the whole panel, so the ViT protocol cannot separate them and the data it selects on leans to the twin. The recommendation rests on 3 things the ViT selection half does not supply: on Swin-S the same protocol picks the attention input by \SwinSelectionHalfTwinGap{} (\cref{sec:swin}), the attention input is the more stable of the 2 across training seeds, and it is the site whose mechanism \cref{sec:mechanism} can state. The numbers in the main text pool both halves.

\subsection{The family split}
\label{sec:results:family}

The basis groups sites into an input-side family and a residual-adjacent family, and the ranking makes the input-side family look like the winner. It is not a property of the families. Pairing within model and holding the operator fixed, the gap between them depends on which operator is held.

With token masking held fixed the gap is \FamilyGapAdaptive{} [\FamilyGapAdaptiveLow{}, \FamilyGapAdaptiveHigh{}] at the deployable rule and \FamilyGapMatched{} [\FamilyGapMatchedLow{}, \FamilyGapMatchedHigh{}] at matched disturbance, where it cannot be separated from 0. With dropout held fixed instead the gap reverses. At matched disturbance it is \FamilyGapDropoutMatched{} [\FamilyGapDropoutMatchedLow{}, \FamilyGapDropoutMatchedHigh{}], which excludes 0 on the other side. At the deployable rule it is \FamilyGapDropoutAdaptive{} [\FamilyGapDropoutAdaptiveLow{}, \FamilyGapDropoutAdaptiveHigh{}]. With dropout the residual-adjacent family is the better one.

A split whose sign depends on the operator is not a fact about sites. Nor do the families group placements that behave alike: per-sample PSU agrees at \AgreementAcrossFamiliesClean{} across the 2 families and at \AgreementWithinInputSideClean{} within the input-side family (\cref{tab:mech-operator-agreement-family}). It is the operator effect of \cref{sec:mechanism:layernorm} read through a grouping that does not hold the operator fixed, which is also what the basis file's own family tags do: under those tags, which mix operators inside each family, the gap is \FamilyGapConfoundedMatched{} [\FamilyGapConfoundedMatchedLow{}, \FamilyGapConfoundedMatchedHigh{}].

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_shift_ladder.pdf}
\caption{Mean AUROC and TPR at the 10\% and 20\% false-positive budgets of PSBD-TM against the achieved clean shift ratio. The dashed line marks the adaptive target of \AdaptiveShiftTarget{}. AUROC barely moves with the target, at \LadderAurocAtZeroSix{} at a shift ratio of \PlacementMatchTarget{} and \LadderAurocAtZeroEight{} at \AdaptiveShiftTarget{}, while the true-positive rate at a fixed false-positive budget keeps rising with it. These readings interpolate each model's ladder to the exact target, which is why they differ slightly from the adaptive rule's, which takes the smallest swept rate past it.

\subsection{The depth profile and the routing, per block}
\label{app:mechanism}

The direction is not present from the start. \Cref{fig:tac-layers} follows its norm, relative to the clean activation's norm, through the 12 blocks on GTSRB, CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet. On the benign model it never exceeds \DirectionBenignPeakRelNorm{}. On a backdoored model it stays near the benign level through the first half of the network, reaches half of its final value between blocks 7 and 9 depending on the attack, and peaks at blocks 9 to 12. The onset comes earlier for the global triggers than for the patch trigger on CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet, and the order reverses on GTSRB, so the depth at which a trigger is written depends on the dataset as well as the trigger.

\subsection{Agreement with Karayal\c{c}{\i}n et al.}
\label{sec:mechanism:picek}

Karayal\c{c}{\i}n et al.~\cite{karayalcin2026backdoor} studied backdoor directions in ViT-B/16 on the same 3 datasets with an overlapping attack set, and our models let us test 3 of their claims. Their removal of the backdoor by projecting the direction out of every weight matrix that writes to the residual stream reproduces on our models. Over \ErasureCheckpoints{} checkpoints it takes attack success from \ErasureAsrBeforeMean{} to \ErasureAsrAfterAllWritesMean{} at a clean-accuracy cost of \ErasureCaBeforeMean{} to \ErasureCaAfterAllWritesMean{}. Editing the last 2 blocks alone leaves \ErasureAsrAfterBlocksOneZeroOneOneMean{} and removing a random direction leaves \ErasureAsrAfterRandomDirectionMean{}, so neither control moves the attack. Blend on CIFAR-100 survives it exactly as they report, which is the \ErasureAsrAfterAllWritesMax{} in that column. The direction is re-created by every block, so removing it means removing it everywhere, which is also why the perturbation that acts in every block wins in \cref{sec:results:depth}. Their observation that distributed triggers reach the class token earlier than patch triggers holds on CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet and reverses on GTSRB. Their data-free weight detector reproduces on WaNet and only in part on BPP, for the reason \cref{tab:direction-ablation} gives, since a score built from coordinates of the head and the weights sees only the projection of a rotated direction. Their projected gradient descent readings reproduce in part, with the high mid-layer cosine between an adversarial shift and the backdoor direction holding for BPP, Blend and LF but not WaNet. \Cref{app:picek} gives the full numbers.

\subsection{Comparison with Karayal\c{c}{\i}n et al. in detail}
\label{app:picek}

Karayal\c{c}{\i}n et al.~\cite{karayalcin2026backdoor} remove a backdoor by projecting the direction of 1 selected layer out of every weight matrix that writes to the residual stream, the patch embedding and every attention and MLP output projection. They report attack success falling from \cited{97.7} to \cited{6.7} percent on average. Blend on CIFAR-100 is their 1 survivor. \Cref{tab:erasure} repeats their procedure on our models with their layer selection rule. It adds 2 controls, the same edit restricted to blocks 10 and 11 and the same edit with a random direction of the same norm.

\IfFileExists{tables/erasure.tex}{\input{tables/erasure}}{}
\FloatBarrier

The removal reproduces. Over \ErasureCheckpoints{} checkpoints attack success falls from \ErasureAsrBeforeMean{} to \ErasureAsrAfterAllWritesMean{} and clean accuracy from \ErasureCaBeforeMean{} to \ErasureCaAfterAllWritesMean{}. Blend on CIFAR-100 stays at \ErasureAsrAfterAllWritesMax{} at both poison rates exactly as they report. Our first attempt had edited only the last 2 blocks and left every attack intact, which the blocks 10 and 11 column now shows to be expected. The direction is re-created by every block, so removing it means removing it everywhere.

Their second claim, that distributed triggers reach the class token earlier than patch triggers, is measured independently in \cref{fig:tac-layers}. It holds on CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet, where the onset of the backdoor direction comes earlier for Blend, BPP and LF than for BadNets. It reverses on GTSRB, which they did not run.

Their third contribution is a data-free detector that scores the classifier head's rows against the early layers' output projections and flags a model when 1 class stands out by more than 3 standard deviations, and it is reported to flag WaNet and BPP reliably and patch triggers never. We ran the rule with their grid over the number of layers and the floor of the standard deviation on \WeightDetectorBackdoored{} of our backdoored models and \WeightDetectorBenign{} benign ones, and \cref{tab:weight-detector} gives every model. On BPP it names the target on \WeightDetectorBppNamed{} of \WeightDetectorBppModels{} models, and it also names the target of the \WeightDetectorLfModels{} LF model. It never names the target of a BadNets, Blend or TaCT model. On the benign models \WeightDetectorBenignFlagMin{} to \WeightDetectorBenignFlagMax{} percent of the grid flags, every flag a false alarm. Their WaNet result reproduces and their BPP result reproduces only in part, and \cref{tab:direction-ablation} says why a weight-space rule reads little.

\subsection{Cost, forward passes, seeds and a 1-pass alternative}
\label{app:cost}

\paragraph{Forward passes and their cost.}
\label{sec:robustness:passes}

PSBD uses $k = 3$ passes. Over all \PassAllCells{} models the mean AUROC of PSBD-TM rises from \PassAllAurocKOne{} at $k = 1$ to \PassAllAurocKTwo{} at $k = 2$ and \PassAllAurocKThree{} at $k = 3$. On the \PassPilotCells{} models whose sweep reached 20 passes it rises from \PassPilotAurocKOne{} to \PassPilotAurocKThree{} at 3 passes and \PassPilotAurocKTwoZero{} at 20, with TPR at a 10\% false-positive budget going from \PassPilotTprOneZeroKOne{} to \PassPilotTprOneZeroKThree{} to \PassPilotTprOneZeroKTwoZero{}. The second and third pass are worth having on both populations. Beyond the third the gain is \PassPilotAurocGainBeyondThree{} AUROC. That is small but not negligible. The remaining models have not been swept past 3 passes, so we carry the original work's $k = 3$ rather than claim it is converged. \Cref{app:passes} plots both curves as the gain over a single pass, which is the comparison 2 populations license.

\IfFileExists{tables/cost.tex}{\input{tables/cost}}{}

The passes are the cost of the defense. Each pass is a full forward pass of the perturbed network, and \cref{tab:cost} gives the measured cost on the A100 we use. At batch size \CostBatchSize{} a plain ViT-B/16 prediction takes \CostPlainSecondsVit{} seconds per input. PSBD at $k = 3$ costs \CostSlowdownKThreeVit{} that. At $k = 20$ it costs \CostSlowdownKTwoZeroVit{}. Swin-S is faster in absolute terms at every $k$ with the same ratios, \CostSlowdownKThreeSwin{} at 3 passes. A deployment therefore trades a 3-fold slowdown for the detector at $k = 3$ and would pay a further 7-fold for the \PassPilotAurocGainBeyondThree{} that $k = 20$ adds.

\paragraph{Seed variance.}
\label{sec:robustness:seeds}

\SeedCells{} backdoored models were trained \SeedsPerCell{} times with different seeds (BadNets, Blend and WaNet on CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet). PSBD-TM has a seed standard deviation of \SeedSdRecommended{} AUROC on average. PSBD-RD has \SeedSdPublished{}, reaching \SeedSdGainMax{} on its worst model. The gain of PSBD-TM over PSBD-RD is \SeedGainMean{} on these models and changes sign on \SeedGainSignFlips{} of \SeedCells{}, all on Blend and WaNet where both placements are near the ceiling. A single seed is therefore a fair reading of PSBD-TM and an unreliable reading of PSBD-RD, which is weaker on average and far less stable. \Cref{app:seeds} lists the per-seed readings.

\paragraph{A 1-pass alternative.}

A cheaper alternative to PSBD from our own work reads the class token through the final LayerNorm and head at every block, a logit lens \cite{nostalgebraist2020logitlens,belrose2023tunedlens} that costs 1 pass. Over the \DepthCells{} compared models it reads \DepthAurocAll{} AUROC against PSBD's \HeadlineAurocAdaptive{}, a paired difference of \DepthMinusPsbdAuroc{} \DepthMinusPsbdAurocCi{} that contains 0. On the hard attacks it beats PSBD at the smallest budget by \DepthMinusPsbdTprHard{} \DepthMinusPsbdTprHardCi{}, and it wins that budget on \DepthTprWins{} of the \DepthCells{} models. It reads \DepthBenignAuroc{} on the benign references, so it raises no signal where there is no backdoor. The 2 statistics are complementary rather than redundant.
