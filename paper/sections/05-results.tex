\section{Detection results on ViT-B/16}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{How the numbers are read}
\label{sec:results:reading}

Every detection number below is read the same way. For 1 backdoored model and 1 placement, the statistic scores every image of the paired clean and triggered sets, and the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) measures how well those scores separate the 2 sets. A value of 1.0 is perfect, 0.5 is a coin, and below 0.5 the scores order the sets the wrong way round. We never flip a score whose AUROC falls below 0.5, since a defender cannot know in advance which way a model will read. Such a model is reported as below chance.

The true-positive rate (TPR) at a false-positive budget is the share of triggered images flagged when the threshold is set on the clean validation set so that the given share of clean images is flagged. TPR at 1\% is the number a deployment cares about.

Each placement is swept over a ladder of perturbation rates, and 2 rules pick the rate to read. The adaptive rule is the PSBD paper's deployment rule, the smallest rate at which the perturbation changes 80\% of the clean validation predictions. The matched rule reads every placement at the rate where it first changes 60\% of them, so that 2 placements are compared at the same disturbance. It exists only for comparisons. A gain is a difference between 2 placements on the same model, averaged over models, and its 95\% interval is a bootstrap over models, so it says how consistent the gain is across the 65 models.

\subsection{Attention-input token masking against post-residual dropout}
\label{sec:results:headline}

The PSBD paper places dropout after each residual connection of a ResNet basic block. A ViT block has no single counterpart to that site, so we followed the idea rather than the letter and applied dropout to the residual stream after each residual add. We call this post-residual dropout. It works, but it is not the best site a ViT offers. Masking whole tokens at the input of every attention block, before its LayerNorm, is the placement we recommend, and \cref{tab:headline} compares the 2 on the 65 backdoored models.

Token masking leads on every row. 2 patterns stand out. The gain grows as the poison rate falls, from +0.069 at 10\% to +0.161 at 1\%. \label{sec:results:operator}

Both axes matter. Moving token masking from the attention input to the MLP input of the same block costs 0.109, so the site matters on its own. Holding the site at the attention input and switching from token masking to noise costs 0.206, so the perturbation matters on its own too.

The surprising row is the last one. At the MLP input the same comparison reverses sign, and noise beats token masking by 0.056. An account that ranks perturbations once for all sites cannot produce a reversal, and neither can one that ranks sites once for all perturbations. \Cref{sec:mechanism:layernorm} explains the reversal through the LayerNorm that sits directly behind the attention input and absorbs additive noise but not masking.

A tempting simplification is to sort the sites into 2 families, those on a sublayer's input and those adjacent to the residual stream, and to credit the family. An earlier reading of our own data, with dropout on CIFAR-10, suggested an input-side advantage of +0.054. On all 65 models, with the perturbation held at token masking, the family gap is +0.005 with an interval from $-$0.010 to +0.023. There is no family effect once the perturbation is matched. The advantage belongs to 1 specific site, the attention input.

\subsection{Ranking of all 18 placements}
\label{sec:results:basis}

\Cref{fig:basis} ranks all 18 placements by mean AUROC at the adaptive rule. Attention-input token masking is second by 0.001 behind token masking on the attention branch output before its residual add, and first at the matched rule. We recommend it over the nominal first because it leads at matched disturbance and because its site has the clean mechanistic reading of \cref{sec:mechanism}. Post-residual dropout is twelfth.

The spread from best to worst placement is 0.19 AUROC. That is larger than any gain a change to the statistic or the threshold would buy on these models, which is the practical content of this section. On a ViT the placement is the method.

\subsection{Token masking against dropout}
\label{sec:results:tokens}

Dropout, as the original work uses it, zeroes individual coordinates of the perturbed tensor. Token masking zeroes whole tokens, with the class token protected and the survivors rescaled. On a ViT these are different perturbations of the same tensor. A coordinate is 1 of the 768 numbers describing a token. It is renormalised by the next LayerNorm and averaged by the next attention, so dropping 30\% of coordinates leaves every token present with a slightly noisier description. A token is the unit that attention routes, so masking it removes that token from every attention pattern of the block.

\Cref{sec:mechanism} shows that a backdoor is carried by the trigger's own tokens for most of the network and that its direction is not aligned with any coordinate axis. Zeroing the 20 coordinates the trigger moves most leaves attack success at 1.000. A coordinate-level perturbation therefore cannot remove the shortcut, while a token-level one can. The detection numbers agree. Dropout at the attention input scores 0.862 on Swin against 0.968 for token masking at the same input (\cref{sec:swin}), and channel masking, which removes whole coordinates, trails token masking by 0.113 on ViT (\cref{tab:operators}).

\subsection{Which blocks to perturb}
\label{sec:results:depth}

Attention-input token masking perturbs all 12 blocks. Restricting it to blocks 5 to 8 or to blocks 9 to 12 costs 0.06 in both cases, so the input-side perturbation works best when it acts everywhere. Dropout before the residual add behaves differently. Restricted to blocks 5 to 8 it gains 0.052 over the all-blocks version, and restricted to blocks 1 to 4 it loses 0.062.

The difference fits the mechanism of \cref{sec:mechanism}. A token mask that acts before routing removes the trigger's tokens at whatever depth routing happens, so every added block is another chance to remove them. A perturbation on the residual stream hits the stream itself, and in early blocks the stream carries mostly clean content, so perturbing it there spends budget on the wrong signal.
